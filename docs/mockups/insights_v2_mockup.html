Maine Scratch EV Least bad today

Ranks every active Maine scratch game by the expected value of a ticket bought today — it cannot predict a win, and every scratch game loses money on average.

▶ How the score works
The score (0–100) compares each game's naive expected value per dollar against every other rateable Maine scratch game today, discounted when the inventory data behind it is thin. That expected value is an upper bound on unclaimed prize value per estimated unsold ticket — some unclaimed prizes sit on tickets already sold but not yet claimed, so the true expected value is lower. Grades are fixed bands on that score. Scores are relative and re-anchor every day — a 95 today is not a 95 tomorrow, and no score predicts a win. Every scratch game loses money on average; the best pick is only the least bad.

Ticket complexity × burn rate

A research view over 11 years of archived lifecycles: does a game's complexity (price, prize structure, play mechanics) travel with how its prize money burns down as tickets sell — and with how much is still outstanding when the game leaves the board? Descriptive patterns only — nothing here predicts a win, and no pattern below says where a winning ticket is.

Complexity vs. burn gap — every usable lifecycle

Each dot is one game lifecycle. Burn gap > 0: unclaimed money lingered above the instant-claim line while tickets sold (slow burn / claim lag). Burn gap < 0: prize money exited faster than sales (fast burn).

What travels with what

Spearman rank correlation with a seeded bootstrap 95% interval. Descriptive associations in archived data — not significance tests, not causes, not predictions.

How to read a burn curve

Unclaimed prize money (share of its start) against inventory sold. The gray diagonal is the instant-claim benchmark; the burn gap is the average height above or below it.

Complexity index — current-era games

Structural components for every game, plus play-mechanic components parsed from the state's own game pages where available. The index is a fixed transform of component z-scores (0–100), not a quality score.

Where published winners bought

Maine names its claimed winners — and where the ticket was sold. This view aligns those public reports into town and retailer trends. Read it as history, never as advice: past winner locations carry zero information about where future winning tickets will be sold. Every unsold ticket is uniformly random, wherever it sits.

Winner purchase towns

Dot size: published winners whose ticket was sold in that town. Dot depth: per-10k-resident rate after empirical-Bayes shrinkage (small samples are pulled toward the statewide mean). Town positions are approximate.

Town trends, shrunk toward the mean

One winner in a tiny town proves nothing, so raw per-capita rates are pulled hard toward the statewide mean before display — a 500-person town's 20×-Portland raw rate collapses to near parity, and its wide whisker says how little that ranking means. Whiskers span the 90% posterior interval; rows with fewer than 3 winners are flagged. Rows are ordered by winner count, not rate.

What this data is

Score, grade, and reason are computed once daily from the state's published unclaimed-prize and percent-unsold figures; claim-lag games are shown separately below the shortlist, never ranked among the picks.

Data: Maine State Lottery · winner reports from the Lottery's Winner Information page

Winner locations describe published past claims only — they carry no information about future tickets; every unsold ticket is uniformly random.

The Maine State Lottery's Official Outstanding Prize List prevails over anything shown here.
